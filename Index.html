How to spot a phishing message

Phishing is a message that pretends to come from someone you trust, such as your bank, mobile money provider, employer or a friend, to trick you into giving up a password, PIN or money. It arrives by SMS, WhatsApp, email or a phone call.

Warning signs

Pressure and urgency. “Your account will be closed in one hour.” Real organizations give you time and several ways to confirm. Scammers want you to act before you think.

Requests for secrets. No genuine bank, mobile money agent or support team will ask for your PIN, password or one-time code. If someone asks, it is a scam, even if they know your name.

Strange links. Look closely at the web address. A link like “mybank-secure-login.example.com” is not the bank’s real domain. When unsure, do not tap the link. Open the official app or type the address yourself.

Too-good-to-be-true offers. Free money, surprise prizes and job offers that ask for a fee up front are classic bait.

What to do

Stop and do not reply. Contact the organization using a number you already trust. Report the message to your network provider or bank. If you already shared a password, change it at once and turn on two-step login. If you shared a PIN, contact your provider immediately.

Passwords that are strong and easy to remember

A strong password is long and unique. Length matters more than clever symbols. Reusing one password everywhere is the biggest risk, because when one website leaks it, attackers try that password on your email and social accounts.

Use a passphrase

Join four or five unrelated words, for example “river-lamp-orange-drum-seven”. It is easy to remember and very hard to guess. Avoid names, birthdays, phone numbers and common words like “password” or “123456”.

One password per important account

Your email deserves its own password above all, because email can reset almost every other account. Protect it first.

Turn on two-step login

Two-step login (also called 2FA) asks for a code in addition to your password. Even if someone steals the password, they are stopped. Use an authenticator app when possible, as SMS codes are weaker.

Keep track safely

A reputable password manager can store many passwords so you only remember one strong passphrase. If you prefer paper, keep it at home in a safe place, never photographed on your phone.

Never share your password or a login code with anyone, including people who claim to be support staff.

Seven steps to secure your Android phone

Your phone holds your money, messages and photos. These steps take about ten minutes and use only built-in settings.

1. Set a screen lock

Use a PIN of six digits or more, or a strong pattern. Add a fingerprint for convenience.

2. Update your phone and apps

Updates fix known security holes. Turn on automatic updates in the Play Store and in system settings.

3. Install apps only from the Play Store

Apps from unknown websites or shared files are the most common way phones get infected. Check the developer name and reviews before installing.

4. Review app permissions

A flashlight app does not need your contacts. In Settings, open Permissions and remove anything an app does not need.

5. Turn on Find My Device

If your phone is lost or stolen, you can locate it, lock it or erase it remotely.

6. Back up your data

Back up photos and contacts to your Google account so a lost phone does not mean lost memories.

7. Be careful on public Wi-Fi

Avoid banking or mobile money on open Wi-Fi networks. Mobile data is safer.

1. Digital Forensics and Digital Evidence Fundamentals

Digital forensics is the science of identifying, preserving, analysing and presenting data from digital devices so it can be used as reliable evidence. It supports criminal cases, corporate investigations, incident response and civil disputes. Whenever a computer, phone, server, camera or network device is involved in an event, there is usually a digital trail, and forensics is the discipline that finds that trail, explains it and defends it.

Branches of digital forensics

The field is wide. Computer forensics examines laptops, desktops and servers. Mobile forensics deals with phones and tablets. Network forensics studies traffic and logs, while memory forensics analyses what is running in RAM. Cloud forensics looks at data held in online services, and malware forensics studies malicious code. Each branch uses different tools, but all share the same principles of preservation, documentation and objectivity.

The investigation process

Most investigations follow clear phases. First comes identification: deciding which devices and data sources may hold relevant information. Next is preservation, where the scene is secured so nothing is changed or lost. Collection follows, using forensically sound methods to make copies. Then the examiner moves to examination and analysis, searching the copies for relevant artifacts and interpreting what they mean. Finally the findings are written into a report and, if needed, presented in court or to management.

The golden rule is never to work on the original evidence. Every action is documented in notes so that another examiner could repeat the steps and reach the same result. This repeatability is what separates forensic work from casual troubleshooting.

What counts as digital evidence

Digital evidence is any information stored or transmitted in digital form that can support or disprove a claim. Examples include documents, spreadsheets, emails, chat messages, photos, videos, browser history, system logs, application data and the metadata attached to all of these. Metadata, meaning data about data, such as creation dates, authors, GPS coordinates and device identifiers, often tells a story that the visible content does not, so it is always preserved together with the file.

Volatile and non-volatile evidence

Evidence is classed by how long it survives. Volatile evidence, such as RAM contents, running processes, open network connections and logged-in users, disappears when the device loses power. Non-volatile evidence, such as hard drives, SSDs, USB drives and memory cards, remains after shutdown. Because volatile data is lost first, investigators follow the order of volatility: collect the most fragile evidence first (memory and network state), then temporary files, then disk data, then logs and backups.

Characteristics of good evidence

For evidence to be accepted it must be authentic (it is what it claims to be), reliable (collected and processed with trusted methods), complete (not selectively presented), and believable to a court. It also must be admissible, which means it was obtained lawfully. Examiners need proper authority such as a warrant, a court order or written consent, and must respect privacy by examining only what the case requires.

Why digital evidence is fragile

Digital data is easy to change, copy and delete, often without leaving obvious signs. Simply turning on a computer can alter hundreds of files and timestamps. Plugging in a drive can modify it. Remote wiping, automatic updates and cloud synchronisation can destroy evidence within minutes. For this reason the first responder's actions matter greatly: do not browse the device, do not shut it down carelessly, photograph the screen, note what is connected and call a trained examiner.

Ethics and professional conduct

Forensic examiners must be impartial. They report what the evidence shows, including facts that do not support the party that hired them. They keep information confidential, stay within the scope of their authority, and admit the limits of their findings. A credible examiner will say "the evidence is consistent with" rather than overstate a conclusion.

In short, digital forensics combines technical skill, legal awareness and disciplined documentation. Master these fundamentals and every later topic in this series, from imaging to malware analysis, becomes easier to understand.

2. Chain of Custody

Chain of custody is the documented record of who handled a piece of evidence, when, where, why, and what was done to it, from the moment of seizure until it is presented in court or returned. It answers one simple question that every judge and opposing lawyer will ask: how do we know this evidence is the same as what was originally found, and that nobody has changed it?

Why it matters

Even a perfect technical analysis can be thrown out if the chain of custody has gaps. If nobody can explain where a laptop was for three days, the other side can argue it was tampered with, and the court may reduce the weight of the evidence or refuse it entirely. In corporate investigations and disciplinary hearings, a poor chain of custody can also make the findings unfair or unusable. A good chain protects the examiner, the organisation and the person under investigation.

Starting at the scene

Custody begins at the scene. The first responder photographs the area and the devices as found, noting what was on or off, what was connected, and what was on the screen. Each item receives a unique identifier, a label with date and time, and a description including make, model and serial number. Cables, chargers and accessories are recorded too, because they may be needed later. Passwords, notes and sticky notes found nearby are documented as potential evidence.

What the custody form records

A chain of custody form usually contains the case number, the item ID and description, the location and date it was collected, and the name of the person who collected it. After that, every transfer is logged with the name and signature of the person releasing the item, the name and signature of the person receiving it, the date and time, and the reason for the transfer. Any examination, copying or testing is recorded, including which tools were used. Forms must be completed at the time of the event, never later from memory, and corrections are made by a single line and initials, never by erasing.

Packaging and storage

Physical evidence is placed in tamper-evident bags or boxes and sealed, with the seal signed and dated across the edge. Electronic devices are protected from static, heat, moisture and magnetic fields. Mobile phones are isolated from networks with airplane mode, a Faraday bag or a shielded container so they cannot be remotely wiped or receive new data. Items are stored in a secure, access-controlled room or locker, and access to that room is logged.

Transporting evidence

During transport, evidence should stay with an identified person or in a locked container, never left unattended in a vehicle. Long journeys and handovers between agencies must be recorded as clearly as any other transfer. If a courier is used, the courier's details and the tracking information form part of the record.

Digital safeguards: hashes and logs

Digital evidence has an extra layer of protection. When a forensic image is created, its hash value is calculated and recorded on the custody form. Whenever the image is later opened, copied or moved, the hash is calculated again. If the values match, the data is mathematically proven to be unchanged. Evidence storage servers and tools also keep access logs, which can support the paper trail.

Working copies

Examiners work from copies of the image, not the master. The master image is stored securely and used only to create new working copies. Each copy is also hashed and logged. This approach means that even if a working copy is damaged, the original evidence remains safe and available.

Common mistakes to avoid

Typical failures include missing signatures, unexplained time gaps, mixing evidence from different cases, using a personal laptop to examine evidence, leaving a device powered on and connected to the network, and storing items in an unlocked office. Another frequent error is changing the device in order to look at it, such as opening files directly on the original drive.

Practical example

Imagine a manager's laptop is seized after a data leak. The investigator photographs it, seals it in a bag with ID 001, and signs the form. The laptop is handed to the lab examiner, who signs for it, records the time, and stores it in a locked cabinet. The drive is imaged and hashed, and the hash is written on the form. The laptop is resealed and returned to storage. Months later, in a hearing, the examiner can show every step and prove nothing was altered.

Chain of custody is not complicated, but it requires discipline. Record everything, record it immediately, and never leave a gap.

3. Forensic Imaging and Hashing

A forensic image is an exact, bit-for-bit copy of a storage device or partition. Unlike a normal file copy, it includes everything on the drive: active files, deleted files, unallocated space, slack space, file system structures and hidden areas.
